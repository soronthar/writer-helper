# Seven-Point Story Structure

## Overview
The Seven-Point Story Structure, developed by author Dan Wells, organizes a narrative around seven named plot points with a defining planning philosophy: **design from the end backward**. Determine your Resolution first, then establish a Hook that contrasts sharply with it. The transformation between Hook and Resolution is the story's promise to the reader. The Midpoint divides the story into two phases — reactive and proactive — and the two Pinch Points apply pressure between the major turning points.

**Best suited for:** Writers who have a character or world but not yet a plot — the backwards-planning method forces clarity about where the story is going before the middle is designed. Tightly plotted genre fiction (thriller, mystery, fantasy, romance), where logical plot progression and clear cause-and-effect are central. Stories where the protagonist's agency is the primary vehicle of the plot.

**Less suited for:** Stories built around ambiguity, mood, or character study where transformation is gradual and resists neat turning points. Non-linear structures.

**Relationship to other structures:** A more plot-specific refinement of Three-Act Structure — both share the beginning-middle-end framework, but Seven-Point provides precise mechanics for the transitions. Less character-psychological than Dan Harmon's Story Circle; more granular than Three-Act alone. The Midpoint's reactive→proactive pivot mirrors Three-Act's Plot Point Two, but placed and named more explicitly. The symmetrical structure (Hook↔Resolution, Plot Turn 1↔Plot Turn 2, Pinch 1↔Pinch 2) makes the design logic unusually legible.

---

## Quick Reference

| # | Point | Position | Purpose |
|---|-------|----------|---------|
| 1 | Hook | 0–5% | Establish protagonist's starting state — the inverse of where they'll end |
| 2 | Plot Turn 1 | ~20–25% | Inciting incident; protagonist is pulled into the adventure |
| 3 | Pinch 1 | ~37% | Antagonist/conflict introduced at full force; stakes made concrete |
| 4 | Midpoint | ~50% | False victory or revelation; protagonist shifts from reactive to proactive |
| 5 | Pinch 2 | ~62% | All seems lost; protagonist's resolve tested at its lowest point |
| 6 | Plot Turn 2 | ~75% | Protagonist uses accumulated knowledge to create their own circumstances |
| 7 | Resolution | ~90–100% | Conflict resolved; transformation promised by the Hook delivered |

**Symmetrical pairs:** Hook↔Resolution (starting state vs. ending state), Plot Turn 1↔Plot Turn 2 (pulled in vs. taking charge), Pinch 1↔Pinch 2 (antagonist's power shown vs. antagonist's power at maximum).

---

## Planning Note: Work Backwards

Before filling in the points in order, answer these two questions first:
1. **What is the Resolution?** — Where does the protagonist end up? What transformation has occurred?
2. **What is the Hook?** — What is the opposite or inverse of the Resolution? What starting state makes the final transformation meaningful by contrast?

Everything between them is the mechanism that moves the protagonist from one to the other. This approach prevents the most common story problem: a vivid opening and a planned ending, with a middle that drifts because neither anchor was designed to require the other.

## Plot Points

### Point 1 — Hook
- **Narrative beat:** Introduce the protagonist in their ordinary state. This state should contrast sharply with who they will be at the Resolution — the Hook establishes the story's starting conditions, and the gap between Hook and Resolution is the transformation the story promises.
- **Story position:** 0–5%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What is the Resolution? (Answer this first — the Hook is designed in relation to it.)
  - What is the protagonist's starting state that makes the Resolution feel like genuine change?
  - What does the protagonist believe, lack, or fear at the Hook that the story will challenge?
  - What is compelling enough about this starting state to make the reader want to follow the journey?
- **Notes:** The Hook and Resolution form the story's core promise. A strong Hook immediately implies what the story is about — not through exposition, but through the specificity of the protagonist's starting conditions. The contrast between Hook and Resolution should be visible in concrete terms: who the protagonist is, what they have, what they believe, how they relate to others.

---

### Point 2 — Plot Turn 1
- **Narrative beat:** An event, discovery, or encounter pulls the protagonist into the story's central conflict. This is the inciting incident — the moment the story truly begins and the protagonist can no longer remain in the world of the Hook.
- **Story position:** ~20–25%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What specific event forces the protagonist into the adventure?
  - Is the protagonist pulled in (external force) or does the protagonist step in (internal choice)? What are the implications of each?
  - What does the protagonist stand to gain by engaging — and what do they risk?
  - How does Plot Turn 1 connect to the transformation the Resolution requires?
- **Notes:** Plot Turn 1 is the mirror of Plot Turn 2. In Plot Turn 1, circumstances act on the protagonist — they respond to something beyond their control. In Plot Turn 2, the protagonist acts on circumstances — they create their own conditions. The contrast between these two points is the story's reactive→proactive shift, made structural. When planning Plot Turn 1, ask: what is the proactive equivalent of this event that will appear at Plot Turn 2?

---

### Point 3 — Pinch 1
- **Narrative beat:** The antagonist or central conflict is introduced at significant force, making the stakes concrete and the obstacles real. This is the first major pressure point — a reminder that the protagonist's goal will not be easily achieved.
- **Story position:** ~37%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What does the antagonist (or the central conflict's full scope) look like when revealed at force?
  - What specific threat or obstacle does Pinch 1 put in the protagonist's path?
  - What does the protagonist now understand about the challenge that they didn't before?
  - How does Pinch 1 raise the cost of pursuing the goal?
- **Notes:** Pinch 1 and Pinch 2 are symmetrical pressure events: Pinch 1 introduces the antagonist's power; Pinch 2 shows it at maximum. Both exist to prevent the story from relaxing into complacency between its major turning points. Pinch 1 should feel like a credible preview of what the protagonist is actually up against — if it underrepresents the antagonist's threat, the Midpoint false victory will ring hollow.

---

### Point 4 — Midpoint
- **Narrative beat:** A false victory, revelation, or crisis at the story's center that marks the hard transition from reactive to proactive protagonist. The protagonist appears to win, or gains critical information — but the conditions of that "victory" contain the seeds of the Pinch 2 crisis.
- **Story position:** ~50%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What does the protagonist appear to win or learn at the Midpoint?
  - Why is this a false victory? What cost or complication is embedded in it that is not yet visible?
  - What does the Midpoint teach the protagonist that enables the proactive action at Plot Turn 2?
  - After the Midpoint, what does the protagonist now *choose* to pursue, rather than simply react to?
- **Notes:** The reactive→proactive shift at the Midpoint is the Seven-Point Structure's most important mechanical feature. Before the Midpoint, the protagonist responds to events; after it, they drive events. This shift should be visible in behavior — the protagonist starts making decisions rather than just surviving. The false victory is critical: a clean success here would deflate the story; the hidden cost is what fuels Pinch 2.

---

### Point 5 — Pinch 2
- **Narrative beat:** The antagonist or central conflict reaches maximum pressure. Everything seems lost — the protagonist's plans have failed, their resources are depleted, and success appears impossible. This is the story's emotional nadir.
- **Story position:** ~62%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What specifically goes wrong? What does the protagonist lose, fail at, or have taken from them?
  - How does this connect directly to the false victory of the Midpoint — what cost is now being collected?
  - What is the protagonist's lowest point emotionally and practically?
  - What does the protagonist still have — some resource, relationship, or quality — that will enable Plot Turn 2?
- **Notes:** Pinch 2 is the despair moment, not the transformation moment. This distinguishes it from the Hero's Journey's Ordeal, which is where transformation occurs. In the Seven-Point Structure, the protagonist has already made their proactive commitment at the Midpoint; Pinch 2 tests whether they can follow through despite maximum adversity. The protagonist should appear to have nothing left — but Plot Turn 2 is earned by showing they do.

---

### Point 6 — Plot Turn 2
- **Narrative beat:** The protagonist, using knowledge, relationships, or capabilities accumulated throughout the story, takes decisive action to create the conditions for the final confrontation. This is not reactive survival — it is the protagonist engineering their own path to the Resolution.
- **Story position:** ~75%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What specific knowledge, skill, or resource does the protagonist use that they did not have at Plot Turn 1?
  - How does this action contrast with Plot Turn 1 — how is the protagonist now driving events rather than responding to them?
  - What does the protagonist do that makes the Resolution possible?
  - What does this action cost, and is the protagonist willing to pay?
- **Notes:** Plot Turn 2 is the structural mirror of Plot Turn 1. The transformation between them is the story's argument: this person, who was pulled into events they couldn't control, has become someone who creates the circumstances of their own resolution. The protagonist's action here must feel earned — it should be possible *only because* of what they experienced and lost between Plot Turn 1 and now.

---

### Point 7 — Resolution
- **Narrative beat:** The central conflict is resolved. The protagonist's transformation — promised by the contrast between the Hook and this moment — is delivered. What the story set out to do is done.
- **Story position:** ~90–100%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - How does the Resolution contrast with the Hook? What is different about the protagonist now?
  - Is the transformation a gain, a loss, or both — and is that ambiguity intentional?
  - What happens to the antagonist or central conflict?
  - What does the world look like in the story's new equilibrium?
- **Notes:** The Resolution is designed first, in this structure — which means by the time it is written, every prior plot point should have been designed to make this specific ending feel inevitable. If the Resolution surprises the writer, some earlier point was probably designed without sufficient connection to the ending. The Resolution does not need to be happy — it needs to be true to the transformation the story promised.

---

## Flexibility

**The backwards-planning method is a design tool, not a writing order:** Plan from the Resolution backward; write from the Hook forward. The planning sequence (Resolution → Hook → Midpoint → Plot Turns → Pinches) does not need to match the drafting sequence.

**Points that can be compressed:**
- Pinch 1 and Plot Turn 1 can be combined in shorter works — the inciting incident that pulls the protagonist in can simultaneously reveal the antagonist's scope
- Resolution and the final beats of Plot Turn 2 can flow together without a distinct break

**Points that should not be skipped:**
- The Midpoint's reactive→proactive shift — without it, the protagonist is passive throughout, and the Resolution feels unearned
- Pinch 2 — without genuine despair before the Resolution, the final success costs nothing
- The Hook/Resolution contrast — if the protagonist ends where they began, the story has no arc

**On symmetry:** The structure's symmetrical design (Hook↔Resolution, Plot Turn 1↔Plot Turn 2, Pinch 1↔Pinch 2) is a planning tool as much as a structural description. When drafting, ask: how does this plot point echo or contrast with its mirror? The symmetry creates the sense of a story that knows where it is going.
