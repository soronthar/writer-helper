# Dan Harmon's Story Circle

## Overview
Dan Harmon's Story Circle is an 8-step circular structure developed by TV writer and showrunner Dan Harmon (*Community*, *Rick and Morty*) and grounded in Jungian psychology and Campbell's monomyth. It frames the story as a circle — a descent from comfort into the unknown and an ascent back, with the character returning changed. Unlike the Hero's Journey, it strips away mythic and genre-specific trappings and focuses entirely on the psychological transformation at the core of all character-driven narrative.

The circle's underlying philosophy: stories exist because communities need members who can go out, adapt, and return with something new. The transformation is not just personal — it serves the group. This is what gives the return its weight.

**Best suited for:** Character-driven stories where inner transformation is the central subject. Contemporary fiction, literary fiction, TV drama, short stories, and any story where what happens externally matters less than what happens internally. Particularly effective for stories with morally ambiguous conclusions or where the "victory" costs the protagonist something permanent.

**Less suited for:** Tightly plotted genre fiction where external plot mechanics are primary. Stories with ensemble casts and no clear protagonist.

**Relationship to other structures:** A direct psychological simplification of the Hero's Journey — the same arc, with the mythic scaffold removed and the character psychology foregrounded. Maps onto Three-Act Structure (steps 1–3 = Act One; steps 4–6 = Act Two; steps 7–8 = Act Three). Its 8-step symmetry makes it more concise than the Hero's Journey's 12 stages and more character-focused than the Seven-Point Structure.

---

## Quick Reference

The circle has two halves. The **descent** (steps 1–4) takes the character from comfort into the unknown. The **ascent** (steps 5–8) returns them — changed. Each step in the descent has a mirror step in the ascent.

| # | Step | Half | Position | Purpose |
|---|------|------|----------|---------|
| 1 | Zone of Comfort | Descent | 0–5% | Establish who the character is in their normal state |
| 2 | Want Something | Descent | ~10% | The desire that will drive them out of comfort |
| 3 | Enter Unfamiliar Situation | Descent | ~20% | Cross the threshold into the unknown |
| 4 | Adapt to It | Descent | 20–50% | Acquire the skills, relationships, or knowledge needed to survive |
| 5 | Get What They Wanted | Ascent | ~55% | Goal achieved — but at a cost not yet fully understood |
| 6 | Pay Heavy Price | Ascent | ~65% | The true cost of the achievement becomes clear |
| 7 | Return to Familiar Situation | Ascent | ~80% | Back to the starting world — but it looks different now |
| 8 | Having Changed | Ascent | ~95–100% | The character is demonstrably not who they were in step 1 |

**Mirror pairs:** 1↔8 (who they are before/after), 2↔7 (want vs. reality of return), 3↔6 (entering the unknown vs. paying for it), 4↔5 (adapting vs. the false victory that adaptation produced).

## The Descent

### Step 1 — Zone of Comfort
- **Narrative beat:** Introduce the character in their ordinary, familiar circumstances. Establish their baseline — how they see themselves, how they relate to their world, what they have and what they lack. This is the state the reader will measure all future change against.
- **Story position:** 0–5%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What does this character's comfortable life look like — and what makes it feel safe or stagnant?
  - What do they believe about themselves and the world at this point?
  - What are they missing or avoiding that the story will eventually force them to confront?
  - Who do they need to be by step 8, and how far is that from who they are now?
- **Notes:** The Zone of Comfort must feel genuinely livable. If the character's starting state is only misery or dissatisfaction, the circle loses its tension — there must be something worth protecting, even if it is also limiting. The contrast between step 1 and step 8 is the story's central emotional event; make the starting point specific and real. Mirror of step 8.

---

### Step 2 — Want Something
- **Narrative beat:** A desire surfaces — something the character needs, hopes for, or is driven toward. This want is what will pull them out of their comfort zone. It may be conscious or unconscious, articulated or intuited.
- **Story position:** ~10%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What does the character want? (Be specific — vague wants produce vague stories.)
  - Is this what they *need*, or is the want a surface expression of a deeper need they haven't yet identified?
  - What makes this want feel urgent or irresistible now, when it wasn't before?
  - What would it cost them to pursue it — and are they willing to pay that?
- **Notes:** The want in step 2 is often not what the character actually gets in step 5 — the circle's power comes from the gap between what was desired and what was achieved. The want should be something specific enough that the reader can hold it clearly in mind for the entire descent. Mirror of step 7.

---

### Step 3 — Enter Unfamiliar Situation
- **Narrative beat:** The character crosses a threshold into a world, situation, or set of circumstances that operates by different rules than the Zone of Comfort. There is no immediate path back.
- **Story position:** ~20%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What is the specific unfamiliar situation — where, with whom, under what conditions?
  - What makes it genuinely unfamiliar? What rules of the character's normal life no longer apply?
  - Is the crossing voluntary, forced, or accidental? How does that affect the character's relationship to the journey?
  - What does the character bring from their Zone of Comfort that will help or hinder them here?
- **Notes:** The threshold crossing should feel irreversible in the moment — even if the character could technically go back, they can't un-know what they've encountered. Mirror of step 6: entering the unfamiliar is what ultimately produces the price the character pays.

---

### Step 4 — Adapt to It
- **Narrative beat:** The character learns to survive and operate in the unfamiliar situation. They acquire new skills, form new relationships, develop new beliefs, or find unexpected capabilities. This is the longest section of the story.
- **Story position:** 20–50%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What specific skills, knowledge, or relationships does the character develop to navigate this world?
  - What do they have to let go of from their Zone of Comfort in order to adapt?
  - What does adaptation cost them — what part of their old self do they lose or compromise?
  - What false conclusion do they draw from their adaptation that step 5 will test?
- **Notes:** Adaptation is not simply competence-building — it involves the character changing who they are, not just what they can do. The changes here must be visible and specific; vague growth produces an unconvincing step 5 victory and a hollow step 6 price. Mirror of step 5: what the character does to adapt is exactly what produces the consequence of getting what they wanted.

## The Ascent

### Step 5 — Get What They Wanted
- **Narrative beat:** The character achieves the goal they set out for in step 2. But this is a false victory — they have what they wanted, but at a cost that is not yet fully apparent, or the achievement reveals that what they wanted was not what they needed.
- **Story position:** ~55%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What does the character get — specifically? Does it match what they wanted in step 2?
  - What has their adaptation (step 4) made possible that also makes this victory costly?
  - What do they *not* yet realize about the price they've paid or will pay?
  - Is this the moment of maximum external success and minimum internal understanding?
- **Notes:** Step 5 is the circle's most misunderstood beat. It is not a true victory — it is the moment before the true cost becomes visible. The character should feel, briefly, that they have succeeded. The reader should feel the same thing, then the unease. This distinction is what separates the Story Circle from simpler structures where the midpoint is straightforwardly a crisis. Mirror of step 4.

---

### Step 6 — Pay Heavy Price
- **Narrative beat:** The true cost of the achievement and the adaptation becomes clear. Something is lost — a relationship, an aspect of identity, a belief, a person. The character cannot have both the thing they got and what they used to be.
- **Story position:** ~65%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What specifically is the price? What does the character lose that they cannot get back?
  - How does the price connect directly to the adaptation in step 4 — what did they do or become that made this loss inevitable?
  - Who or what bears the cost alongside the character? (The price often falls partly on others.)
  - Does the character understand, at this point, the full weight of what they've lost?
- **Notes:** The price must be real and specific — a vague "things are harder now" is not a price. The most powerful step 6 prices are ones where the character can clearly see the causal chain: I wanted this, I became this to get it, and becoming this cost me that. Mirror of step 3: the unfamiliar situation they entered is what ultimately extracted this price.

---

### Step 7 — Return to Familiar Situation
- **Narrative beat:** The character re-enters the world they left — physically, socially, or emotionally. But the Zone of Comfort looks different now, because they are different. They cannot fully return to who they were.
- **Story position:** ~80%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What does the return look like — is it a physical homecoming, a resumption of old relationships, or a re-encounter with the beliefs and circumstances of step 1?
  - What is the same about the Zone of Comfort, and what is different — or what does the character perceive differently?
  - What does the character now want, compared to what they wanted in step 2?
  - Who has the character become — and is the Zone of Comfort still a place they belong?
- **Notes:** The return is not a happy homecoming — it is a test of whether the transformation is real. If the character can slip back into exactly who they were, the circle didn't work. The familiar situation should feel both recognizable and subtly wrong — the same room, seen through different eyes. Mirror of step 2: the want that drove the character out is now answered, complicated, or replaced.

---

### Step 8 — Having Changed
- **Narrative beat:** The story confirms, explicitly or implicitly, who the character has become. They are demonstrably not who they were in step 1. The change should be visible in their actions, choices, or relationships — not just stated.
- **Story position:** ~95–100%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What specific action, choice, or moment demonstrates that the character has changed?
  - How does this contrast directly with who they were in step 1? (The mirror should be clear.)
  - What do they carry back to their community — literally or metaphorically — from the journey?
  - Is the change a gain, a loss, or both? Does the story ask the reader to judge it?
- **Notes:** Step 8 is not an epilogue or a summary — it is the story's final beat, and it should show rather than tell. The contrast with step 1 must be visible in behavior, not just stated in narration. The Story Circle's underlying claim is that change earned through the descent is always worth something, even when it is painful — step 8 is where that claim is either made or questioned. Mirror of step 1.

## Flexibility

**The circle can run at multiple scales:** A full novel follows the circle across the whole book; individual chapters or episodes can each run their own smaller circle, with the character in a different zone of comfort at the start of each.

**Steps that can be compressed:**
- Steps 1–2 (Comfort + Want) can be combined in a fast-opening story — establish comfort and desire in the same scene
- Steps 7–8 (Return + Changed) often flow together without a clear break

**Steps that should not be collapsed:**
- Steps 5 and 6 (Get What They Wanted + Pay Heavy Price) — the distinction between false victory and true cost is the structure's most important beat; collapsing them produces a flat emotional resolution
- Steps 1 and 8 — the mirror between who the character was and who they are must be legible to the reader; if both ends look the same, the circle hasn't turned

**On ambiguity:** The Story Circle does not require a positive transformation. The character can return having changed for the worse, or having changed in ways the story refuses to judge as good or bad. What matters is that they have *genuinely* changed — that the circle has turned.
