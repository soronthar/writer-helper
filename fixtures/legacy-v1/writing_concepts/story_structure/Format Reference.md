# Format Reference

This file defines the four story formats used in the Writing Room framework. Use it when selecting a format for a concept, deciding how many chapters to plan, choosing a compatible story structure, or calibrating scope for subplots, characters, and pacing.

## Short Story

**Word count:** 1,500–7,500 words · **Chapters:** 1–3 (or unchaptered)

### Structural fit
The [[Fichtean Curve]] is the most natural fit: it starts in conflict with no setup phase, which is essential when word count is limited. The [[Three-Act Structure]] works when compressed aggressively — Act One can be a single scene. [[Freytag's Pyramid]] suits tragic shorts where the fall is the point.

[[The Hero's Journey]], [[Dan Harmon's Story Circle]], and the [[Seven-Point Story Structure]] are generally too expansive for short fiction — their stage counts create pacing problems at this length.

### Arc requirements
One central conflict. One emotional turn. No room for subplot development — any secondary thread must resolve within the main arc or be implied offscreen. The protagonist's internal state at the end must differ from the beginning, even if only slightly.

### Pacing
Conflict must be present or implied in the first paragraph. There is no space for an establishing ordinary world. Background is delivered through the conflict, not before it.

### Notes for the Outliner
Map the full chosen structure to 1–3 chapters. Adjacent stages (e.g., Call to Adventure + Refusal in the Hero's Journey) compress into a single scene. An unchaptered short story should still be outlined by stage, treating the whole piece as one long chapter.

## Novelette

**Word count:** 7,500–17,500 words · **Chapters:** 3–8

### Structural fit
[[Three-Act Structure]] is the cleanest fit: the word count aligns naturally with its three-phase shape. The [[Fichtean Curve]] works well for character-driven novelettes where the accumulating crises are the point. [[Seven-Point Story Structure]]'s seven beats map well onto 5–7 chapters.

[[The Hero's Journey]] is tight but possible at the high end of the word count range (15,000+). [[Dan Harmon's Story Circle]] is a compact variant of the Hero's Journey and slightly more manageable. [[Freytag's Pyramid]] suits tragic novelettes.

### Arc requirements
One main conflict with room for one subplot thread. The subplot must connect to the main arc — it cannot be self-contained. The protagonist's character arc must be visible, though it can be slight. Every named character should have a clear function.

### Pacing
Setup may occupy at most one chapter before the central conflict is established. The reader needs to understand the stakes by the end of Chapter 1.

### Notes for the Outliner
3–8 chapters; one structure stage per chapter is typical at this length. Avoid running the Hero's Journey unless you are at the upper end of the word count — it needs more room than most novelettes can give it.

## Book

**Word count:** 50,000–100,000 words · **Chapters:** 15–30

### Structural fit
All six named structures are viable at book length. Choose based on story type:

| Structure | Best for |
|-----------|----------|
| [[The Hero's Journey]] | Adventure, fantasy, coming-of-age, transformation arcs |
| [[Three-Act Structure]] | Genre fiction, thrillers, romance, any plot-driven narrative |
| [[Seven-Point Story Structure]] | Plot-focused stories where each beat needs a chapter-level payoff |
| [[Dan Harmon's Story Circle]] | Character-driven books; stories where returning changed is the point |
| [[Fichtean Curve]] | Literary fiction; books where character is revealed through sustained pressure |
| [[Freytag's Pyramid]] | Tragedies; books with downbeat or ambiguous endings |

### Arc requirements
A full protagonist arc. 2–4 subplots, each with its own mini-arc or at minimum a resolution beat. Every subplot should connect to the main arc thematically or causally — avoid subplots that exist only as variety. Secondary characters need enough presence to matter when they resolve.

### Pacing
1–2 chapters of setup is acceptable. The inciting incident must land by Chapter 3 at the latest. The midpoint (whatever the chosen structure places there) must represent a genuine, irreversible shift.

### Notes for the Outliner
15–30 chapters. Group structure stages into chapter clusters rather than assigning one stage per chapter — the Hero's Journey's 12 stages across 20 chapters means most stages span 1–3 chapters, with the long middle section (Tests, Allies, Enemies) spanning many. Annotate each chapter in the outline with its stage label so the Planner and Reviewer-Consistency have a shared reference.

## Book Series

**Word count:** 50,000–100,000 words per book · **Chapters:** 15–30 per book · **Books:** 2–7 typical

### Structural fit
Per-book structure follows the same guidance as Book above. At the series level, [[The Hero's Journey]] and [[Three-Act Structure]] are the most commonly used frameworks for planning the overarching arc — both scale well to multi-book scope.

### Two arcs required
A Book Series concept must capture two distinct arcs:

1. **Per-book arc** — each book has its own satisfying central conflict that resolves by the end. A reader should be able to finish Book 2 without feeling cheated, even if the series arc is unresolved.
2. **Series arc** — a longer-running conflict, question, or transformation that advances each book but resolves only at the series end. This is what makes the series feel like one work rather than a collection.

The `CONCEPT.md` for a Book Series must address both. The **Premise** section should describe the series-level premise; the **Notes** section should describe the first book's specific arc and how it fits the series.

### Arc requirements
Same as Book for each individual volume. Additionally: the series arc must advance in every book — a book that leaves the series arc completely static feels like filler. Subplots may carry across book boundaries if they serve the series arc; subplots that are purely per-book should resolve within that book.

### Character roster
The shared cast grows across books. Early books should introduce only as many characters as the plot requires; resist the urge to populate the full roster in Book 1. Characters introduced in later books need establishment time the earlier books did not need.

### Pacing
Series-level pacing is as important as per-book pacing. Map the major series arc beats across the planned number of books before outlining any individual book. A 4-book series running [[Three-Act Structure]] at the series level places the series midpoint at the end of Book 2 — the irreversible shift that changes everything going forward.

### Notes for the Outliner
Outline one book at a time, but always with the series arc visible. Maintain a separate series-level outline document tracking: which series arc beats are hit each book, which characters are introduced and when, and which threads carry across book boundaries. Do not resolve the series arc's central question until the final book.

## Notes for the Story Ideator

When a format decision affects the concept:
- Cite the format's recommended chapter range in the concept's **Notes** section so the Outliner has a target
- For Book Series, ensure the **Premise** captures the series-level story and the **Notes** section identifies what the first book specifically covers
- If the user requests a format that conflicts with their other constraints (e.g., Hero's Journey for a short story), flag the tension and suggest either adjusting the format or the structure
