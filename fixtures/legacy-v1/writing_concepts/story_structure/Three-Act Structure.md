# Three-Act Structure

## Overview
The Three-Act Structure is the most fundamental and widely used framework in Western storytelling. It divides a narrative into Setup (Act One), Confrontation (Act Two), and Resolution (Act Three), with named plot points marking the transitions between them. Its origins trace to Aristotle's *Poetics* and were formalized for modern screenwriting by Syd Field in *Screenplay* (1979).

**Best suited for:** Nearly any genre or form — this is the baseline structure most readers unconsciously expect. Particularly useful for troubleshooting pacing problems in a draft, or for writers who need a flexible but reliable scaffold. Commercial fiction, genre fiction, drama, and literary fiction all use it in some form.

**Less suited for:** Non-linear narratives, fragmented or experimental structures, or stories deliberately built around subverting dramatic expectation.

**Relationship to other structures:** The Hero's Journey maps almost exactly onto Three-Act; the Journey's value is its granularity (12 stages vs. 3 acts). The Seven-Point Story Structure is a more plot-specific refinement of Three-Act. Dan Harmon's Story Circle is a character-psychology reinterpretation of the same arc.

## Quick Reference

| #   | Stage             | Act                 | Position | Purpose                                                            |

| 3   | Plot Point One    | One                 | ~25%     | Protagonist commits to the journey; Act One ends                   |

## Act One — The Setup

### Stage 1 — Exposition
- **Narrative beat:** Introduce the protagonist in their ordinary world. Establish who they are, what they want, what they lack, and what their world looks like before the story's events change it. Ground the reader in character and setting before the disruption arrives.
- **Story position:** 0–10% — the opening.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - Who is the protagonist and what do they want at the start of the story?
  - What is their ordinary world — and what is comfortable or valuable about it?
  - What flaw, wound, or false belief do they carry that the story will eventually challenge?
  - What tone and genre contract does the opening establish for the reader?
- **Notes:** Exposition is not backstory dumping — it is active scene-setting. Every detail shown in the Exposition should either establish character, set up a later payoff, or establish the contrast with how things will be at the end. Modern pacing tends to keep this short; the Inciting Incident should arrive quickly.

---

### Stage 2 — Inciting Incident
- **Narrative beat:** An event, discovery, or arrival disrupts the protagonist's ordinary world and forces a response. This is the story's ignition point — everything that follows traces back to it.
- **Story position:** ~12% — late in the opening sequence.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What specific event triggers the story? Is it external (something happens to the protagonist) or internal (the protagonist makes a discovery or choice)?
  - What does it demand of the protagonist — what must they now do, decide, or confront?
  - What would happen if they did nothing? (This establishes stakes.)
  - How does it connect to the protagonist's established want or flaw?
- **Notes:** The Inciting Incident should feel both surprising and inevitable — unexpected, but something the reader can see the seeds of in the Exposition. It is not the same as Plot Point One; the Incident disrupts the world, while Plot Point One is when the protagonist commits to responding to that disruption.

---

### Stage 3 — Plot Point One
- **Narrative beat:** The protagonist makes a definitive commitment to engage with the story's central conflict. This is the point of no return — Act One ends, and the story's world of active confrontation begins.
- **Story position:** ~25% — the Act One/Act Two boundary.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What decision or action makes the protagonist's engagement with the conflict irreversible?
  - What do they leave behind — literally or symbolically — when they cross this threshold?
  - What is the protagonist's goal as they enter Act Two? (It may differ from what they ultimately need.)
  - What does the reader now expect the story to be about?
- **Notes:** Plot Point One should feel like a door closing. Even if the protagonist didn't choose it consciously, the story must make clear that retreat is no longer possible. This is one of the most important structural joints in the story — if it's weak, Act Two has no momentum.

---

## Act Two — The Confrontation

### Stage 4 — Rising Action
- **Narrative beat:** The protagonist pursues their goal in an unfamiliar world, encountering escalating obstacles, potential allies, and antagonists. Each challenge should be harder than the last. The protagonist develops new capabilities but also makes mistakes and accumulates costs.
- **Story position:** 25–50% — Act Two, first half.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What are the three or four main obstacles the protagonist faces in this section?
  - Who becomes an ally, and what does each ally contribute to the protagonist's ability to achieve the goal?
  - Who or what is the primary antagonist, and what makes them a genuine threat?
  - What new skill, knowledge, or quality does the protagonist develop?
  - What does the protagonist still misunderstand about the situation or about themselves?
- **Notes:** The Rising Action is where most "saggy middle" problems originate. Keep momentum by ensuring each obstacle escalates meaningfully — not just physically harder, but more personally costly. Subplots and secondary character arcs can be developed here, but they should all connect to the main conflict thematically or causally.

---

### Stage 5 — Midpoint
- **Narrative beat:** A major development at the story's center raises the stakes significantly and shifts the story's direction. This is often a false victory (the protagonist appears to succeed but doesn't fully), a revelation that recontextualizes everything, or a crisis that forces a new approach.
- **Story position:** ~50% — the exact center.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What specific event or revelation occurs at the midpoint?
  - How does it raise the stakes — what is now at risk that wasn't before?
  - What does the protagonist now know, or think they know, that changes their approach?
  - How does it shift the story from its first-half direction to a new second-half direction?
- **Notes:** The Midpoint is not simply the middle of the story — it is an active turning point with real consequences. A story without a strong Midpoint often feels like it has a flat, structureless Act Two. The Midpoint and the Climax should mirror each other: the Midpoint presents the problem in its full scope; the Climax resolves it.

---

### Stage 6 — Plot Point Two
- **Narrative beat:** The protagonist, after a period of reactive struggle, makes a decisive choice to take direct, proactive action against the antagonist. This is the transition from being driven by events to driving them. Act Two ends here.
- **Story position:** ~75% — the Act Two/Act Three boundary.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What specific realization or event triggers the protagonist's shift from reactive to proactive?
  - What do they decide to do, and why is this decision different from everything they've tried before?
  - What do they risk by taking this action? What do they stand to lose?
  - How does this decision reflect their transformation from who they were in the Exposition?
- **Notes:** Plot Point Two is often the story's "lowest point" just before the turn — the protagonist has lost something, failed, or been forced to confront their flaw directly. The decision to act despite this is what makes it meaningful. If Plot Point Two is missing or weak, the Climax will feel unearned.

---

## Act Three — The Resolution

### Stage 7 — Pre-Climax
- **Narrative beat:** The protagonist moves toward the final confrontation, but the antagonist's full strength is revealed and doubt reaches its peak. The protagonist may appear overmatched, isolated, or still carrying an unresolved flaw.
- **Story position:** 75–85%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What does the protagonist face in the lead-up to the Climax that makes success seem doubtful?
  - What is the antagonist's maximum power or threat level here?
  - Has the protagonist fully completed their inner transformation? If not, what is the final obstacle?
  - What must the protagonist accept, sacrifice, or resolve before they can face the Climax?
- **Notes:** The Pre-Climax is about building dread and anticipation, not action. Slow the pace deliberately to let both protagonist and reader confront the weight of the final confrontation. This is the last moment where failure feels genuinely possible.

---

### Stage 8 — Climax
- **Narrative beat:** The central conflict reaches its decisive confrontation. The protagonist faces the antagonist — or the final manifestation of the story's core conflict — and the outcome is determined. The story's main dramatic question is answered here.
- **Story position:** ~85–90%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What is the specific form of the final confrontation? Who or what does the protagonist face?
  - How does the protagonist's transformation over the course of the story make it possible for them to win (or lose, or choose differently) now?
  - What does the protagonist have to risk or give up in this moment?
  - What is the outcome — and what does it mean for the story's themes?
- **Notes:** The Climax should be the story's most intense scene, but "intense" does not always mean physical. A quiet scene where the protagonist makes a definitive moral choice can be a more powerful Climax than a battle. The key requirement: the outcome must feel both surprising and inevitable, earned by everything that came before.

---

### Stage 9 — Denouement
- **Narrative beat:** The story settles into its new equilibrium. Loose ends are resolved, secondary characters get closure, and the reader sees what the world looks like after the Climax's events. The protagonist's transformation is confirmed.
- **Story position:** 90–100%.
- **Questions to answer:**
  - What has changed in the world as a result of the story's events?
  - How is the protagonist different from who they were in the Exposition — and how is that difference visible?
  - Which secondary character threads need closure, and what does each resolution say?
  - What, intentionally, is left unresolved — and is that ambiguity meaningful?
- **Notes:** The Denouement should feel like earned rest, not a rushed ending or a prolonged one. It confirms the transformation the story promised. Short is often better; once the Climax has resolved the central conflict, linger only long enough to let the emotional resonance land.

## Flexibility

**On Act Two length:** Act Two is deliberately the longest act (50% of the story) because confrontation and development require space. Some writers find it easier to treat Act Two as two separate halves split at the Midpoint — creating a de facto four-act structure with acts at 25% / 25% / 25% / 25%.

**Stages that can be compressed:**
- Stages 1–2 (Exposition + Inciting Incident) can merge in fast-opening stories or in medias res beginnings
- Stage 7 (Pre-Climax) can be minimal in shorter works

**Stages that should not be skipped:**
- Stage 3 (Plot Point One) — without it, Act Two has no launch point
- Stage 6 (Plot Point Two) — without it, the Climax is not earned
- Stage 8 (Climax) — without it, there is no story

**On the reactive→proactive shift:** The shift from reactive (stages 1–5) to proactive (stages 6–8) is the structure's defining feature and its most important through-line. Every stage should be evaluated against this arc: is the protagonist still being driven by events, or beginning to drive them?
